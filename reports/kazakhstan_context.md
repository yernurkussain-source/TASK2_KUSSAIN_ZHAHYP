# Kazakhstan has not yet measured the link between GenAI use and procrastination

No verified study was found that relates the frequency of generative AI (GenAI) use to procrastination among university students in Kazakhstan. The result from the Sharma et al. (2026) data, ρ = 0.28 [0.18, 0.38], therefore cannot be carried over to Kazakhstan as an established fact. It should be treated as a starting hypothesis to be tested. That hypothesis has a solid basis: international studies from 2024–2026 report positive associations between GenAI use, dependence or overreliance and procrastination of about r/β = .23–.43. The closest point of comparison, Abbas, Jam & Khan (2024) with β = 0.309, lies inside the Indian confidence interval. However, the most rigorous design, a three-wave panel that controls for baseline procrastination (Zhao et al., 2026), found that use frequency does not predict later procrastination (β = 0.007). A ρ of about .3 therefore more likely reflects stable individual differences that go together than an effect of GenAI on procrastination. The Kazakhstani context differs in three ways. First, GenAI is widespread and promoted by the state: in 2024, 65% of students used it weekly, the AI-Sana programme reached more than 600,000 students, and AI courses are compulsory. Second, academic integrity rules are left to universities, which rely on detectors and originality thresholds. Third, teaching takes place in three languages. Measurement is also an obstacle: there is no Kazakh or Russian version of the Unintentional Procrastination Scale (UPS), and the only Kazakhstani procrastination study whose content could be checked is a 2021 KazNU study with n = 72. A follow-up study should adapt and validate the UPS in Kazakh and Russian, measure the type of use (learning support versus handing the task over to AI) and dependence as well as frequency, run several waves at several universities, and be planned with a sample large enough to detect a weak effect such as ρ ≈ .18.

## 65% of students use GenAI weekly, but procrastination has not been measured

About eight empirical sources on Kazakhstan published in 2024–2026 were found. Only one of them reports an overall use-frequency figure. Zhunusbekova & Askarkyzy (2024) surveyed 840 bachelor's students at three large universities in May 2024: **90% knew ChatGPT and 65% used AI tools for study at least once a week** ([Pedagogy and Psychology](https://journal-pedpsy.kaznpu.kz/index.php/ped/article/view/1801)). This figure was checked at abstract level only. The exact wording of the question and the sampling method are unknown, and the item measures "AI tools" in general rather than ChatGPT. Later small samples show that use is practically universal. In Yrymbayeva et al. (2026), 76 of 77 teacher-education students used AI in their coursework ([Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1809360/full)). In KISI's national survey of 8,000 people, **76.2%** of 18–29-year-olds use AI tools, and in this age group the most common purpose is study and self-development (48.7%) ([Kazinform](https://www.inform.kz/ru/kazahstantsi-vse-aktivnee-obrashayutsya-k-chatgpt-i-gemini-opros-kisi-000f7a)). These data suggest that GenAI is at least as widespread among Kazakhstani students as in the Indian sample, but the measures differ, so a direct comparison is not possible. No Kazakhstani study was found that reports daily use or separate weekly figures for ChatGPT, Gemini or Copilot.

On purposes of use, two Kazakhstani sources give different pictures. In the 2024 multi-university sample the main purposes were **essay writing (35%)**, problem solving (25%) and idea generation (18%) ([Pedagogy and Psychology](https://journal-pedpsy.kaznpu.kz/index.php/ped/article/view/1801)). In the Yrymbayeva et al. (2026) sample, by contrast, most students used AI to summarise readings they had checked (58/77, 75.3%) and to generate ideas or outlines (52/77, 67.5%). Eight students (10.4%) used AI as their main information source, and only 6 (7.8%) submitted AI text with little editing ([Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1809360/full)); these percentages were calculated from the counts in the article. The difference should not be read as a trend, because the question format (single versus multiple answers), discipline, timing and social desirability could each explain it. Still, it points to a distinction that matters for the project. Essay writing and submitting lightly edited text are forms of handing the task over to AI (offloading) and are the most plausible link to procrastination mechanisms. Summarising and idea generation are forms of learning support. In a trilingual system (Kazakh, Russian, English), translation is likely an important purpose as well, but this is an inference only: translation and programming have not been measured as separate purposes in Kazakhstani sources.

Most importantly, **no study in Kazakhstan was found that relates GenAI use to procrastination, time management or self-regulated learning**. The closest evidence does not point in one direction. In a study of 110 Nazarbayev University undergraduates, Aubakirov (2026) reports that AI-use frequency, the share of assignments done with AI and self-reported dependence were not significantly related to GPA. The strongest predictor was prior academic ability (SAT/NUET) ([NeurIPS 2026](https://neurips.cc/virtual/2026/175377)). The page gives no effect sizes, and the publication type (workshop or poster) has not been checked. In a master's thesis at the NU Graduate School of Education, a quasi-experiment with 52 undergraduates at one Kazakhstani university found that ChatGPT use was initially negatively correlated with critical thinking and that scores improved after targeted training ([NU Repository](https://nur.nu.edu.kz/items/b3f800c9-a837-46ca-beef-57acd77fcad9/full)). The 334 TEFL students in Bekturova et al. (2025) named plagiarism and loss of writing ability as risks ([Education and Information Technologies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10639-024-13306-6)). Among 328 first-year students at Astana IT University, **70.1% (95% CI 63.4–76.2) of the 204 who had not used counselling preferred ChatGPT to a psychologist** ([Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1838533/pdf)). This pattern fits a simple reading of the Indian result: frequency alone is only weakly related to outcomes, while *how* AI is used (with or without guidance, with dependence, with little editing) matters much more.

Regional parallels support this reading. Zaripova et al. (2026) studied 381 students at the Tashkent University of Information Technologies and found that the outcome was predicted by self-regulation (β = 0.279) and by ethics and academic autonomy (β = 0.446), while **AI use was not significant (β = 0.030, p = .399)** ([Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1846709/full)). A small survey in Uzbekistan (n = 200), read at abstract level, reports that more than 60% of students use AI frequently ([Zenodo](https://zenodo.org/records/16939406)). A media poll reporting 87% ChatGPT use gives no sample size and contains internal inconsistencies, so its reliability is low ([YuzNews](https://yuz.uz/ru/news/chatgpt-bolshe-ne-drug-studenta)). No such studies were found for Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan or Turkmenistan, and no study in Central Asia has measured GenAI use together with procrastination.

## The state promotes AI and leaves integrity rules to universities

Two national documents govern AI, but neither addresses students' use of GenAI in coursework. The Concept for the Development of Artificial Intelligence for 2024–2029 was approved by Government resolution on 24 July 2024 ([Zakon.kz](https://www.zakon.kz/pravo/6443110-o-chem-idet-rech-v-kontseptsii-razvitiya-iskusstvennogo-intellekta-opublikovan-tekst.html)). The resolution number (No. 592) comes only from a title in a regulation database, and its content was not checked ([regulations.ai](https://regulations.ai/regulations/RAI-KZ-NA-CDAI2XX-2024)). The education content of the Concept sits under "Human capital": free online AI courses, AI modules for different specialties, hackathons and olympiads. The Concept also plans a large language model for Kazakh (KazLLM) and further development of the "Tilmash" translator ([Zakon.kz](https://www.zakon.kz/pravo/6443110-o-chem-idet-rech-v-kontseptsii-razvitiya-iskusstvennogo-intellekta-opublikovan-tekst.html)). Law No. 230-VIII "On Artificial Intelligence" was signed on 17 November 2025 and entered into force on 18 January 2026 ([Stones Law](https://www.stoneslaw.net/kazakhstan-enacts-comprehensive-ai-legal-framework-effective-january/)). It classifies AI systems by risk, requires labelling of synthetic content, and bans manipulation and social scoring ([The Astana Times](https://astanatimes.com/2025/11/kazakhstan-adopts-central-asias-first-ai-law/)). A work created with AI is protected by copyright only when a human made a creative contribution. **The labelling duty falls on system owners and operators, not on students** ([Stones Law](https://www.stoneslaw.net/kazakhstan-enacts-comprehensive-ai-legal-framework-effective-january/)). The articles of the law were not compared with the official text on adilet.zan.kz.

The position of the Ministry of Science and Higher Education (MSHE) permits GenAI, but it has not been set out in a binding order. The ministry stated that it does not support a complete ban on generative neural networks ([Times of Central Asia](https://timesca.com/ai-in-kazakh-universities/)). If AI is used, it must be cited, and universities themselves set the rules for checking plagiarism and AI use ([Kazinform](https://www.inform.kz/ru/chelovek-ili-iskusstvenniy-intellekt-kak-v-kazahstane-proveryayut-avtorstvo-7b6554)). According to the ministry's written reply of May 2025, use of AI in course and diploma work must be agreed with the supervisor. A student may show how AI affected the result, but this is not stated as an obligation, and no sanctions are mentioned ([Baq.kz](https://rus.baq.kz/v-minnauki-rk-obyasnili-kak-dolzhny-ispolzovat-ii-studenty_300014499/)). The status of the Inter-University Standard on the use of AI in higher and postgraduate education is disputed. The MSHE calls it "approved" in 2024 ([Baq.kz](https://rus.baq.kz/v-minnauki-rk-obyasnili-kak-dolzhny-ispolzovat-ii-studenty_300014499/)), while Kazinform describes it as a recommendatory draft prepared by the educational and methodological association at IITU ([Kazinform](https://www.inform.kz/ru/chelovek-ili-iskusstvenniy-intellekt-kak-v-kazahstane-proveryayut-avtorstvo-7b6554)). No order number or registration details for the standard were found. Amendments to the State Compulsory Educational Standard made AI courses a mandatory part of all programmes ([bilim.expert](https://www.bilim.expert/en/post/dinara-shcheglova-ai-sana-shaping-a-new-paradigm-for-higher-education)). Their content covers how AI services work, academic integrity and personal-data protection ([Silk Way TV](https://silkwaytv.kz/en/ai-courses-included-in-mandatory-university-programs-in-kazakhstan_68516)).

As a result, rules differ sharply between universities. Nazarbayev University has had a GenAI policy since 18 October 2023, but only its abstract was read ([NU Regulations](https://regulations.nu.edu.kz/handle/123456789/1030)). Al-Farabi Kazakh National University regulates AI use in its 2025–2026 academic policy but keeps an **originality requirement of at least 75%** for theses. The regulations of Narxoz and Yessenov universities were reported to contain no reference to AI as of May 2026 ([Times of Central Asia](https://timesca.com/ai-in-kazakh-universities/)). In spring 2025 Antiplagiat.Kazakhstan launched a tool that detects AI-generated text. Kazakh National Medical University spent about $27,000 and Toraighyrov University about $19,000 on such purchases ([Times of Central Asia](https://timesca.com/ai-in-kazakh-universities/)). An official statement that detectors cannot be indisputable evidence of academic misconduct is attributed to the "Ministry of Education". This may be the ministry responsible for schools rather than the MSHE, so it should not be cited as the MSHE's position ([Times of Central Asia](https://timesca.com/ai-in-kazakh-universities/)).

State promotion is large in scale. The MSHE's AI-Sana programme covered 118 universities, reached more than 600,000 students in its first stage, and **671,557 certificates** were verified ([bilim.expert](https://www.bilim.expert/en/post/dinara-shcheglova-ai-sana-shaping-a-new-paradigm-for-higher-education)). This is close to the total student body of about 624,500 in the 2024/25 academic year ([24.kz](https://24.kz/ru/news/obrazovanie-i-nauka/702285-kazakhstanskie-vuzy-privlekli-rekordnoe-kolichestvo-studentov)). A memorandum signed with OpenAI on 6 November 2025 provides 165,000 free ChatGPT Edu licences: 62,800 for administrators and university teaching staff and 100,000 for teachers in preschool organisations, schools and vocational colleges; **no licences are allocated to students** ([The Astana Times](https://astanatimes.com/2026/05/kazakhstan-moves-early-on-ai-in-education-through-openai-partnership)). Language is also an issue. ISSAI KAZ-LLM was released on 10 December 2024 in 8B and 70B versions, and its developers describe it as a tool for developing GenAI for "lower-resourced languages" ([ISSAI](https://issai.nu.edu.kz/2024/12/10/official-release-of-the-issai-kaz-llm-open-source-model-is-available/)). No independent evaluation of ChatGPT's quality in Kazakh and no official statistics on students by language of instruction were found. One more fact matters for the comparison: in 2024/25, the largest group among Kazakhstan's 28,500 foreign students came from **India (9,300)** ([24.kz](https://24.kz/ru/news/obrazovanie-i-nauka/702285-kazakhstanskie-vuzy-privlekli-rekordnoe-kolichestvo-studentov)).

This context changes what frequency means. When AI courses are compulsory and new university KPIs measure AI adoption ([Otyrar.kz](https://otyrar.kz/2026/04/vuzy-rk-perehodyat-na-novye-standarty-obucheniya-po-iskusstvennomu-intellektu/)), a student's frequent GenAI use is more likely to reflect programme requirements than a personal avoidance strategy. Originality thresholds and detectors may push students to paraphrase AI text, which in turn affects time management. Both mechanisms are inferences from the data in the sources; nobody has tested them yet. Public opinion is also divided: in a 2025 IPP survey (n = 2,000), 40.5% of adults viewed AI in education positively and 37.4% negatively, and 21.7% think AI makes learning "too easy". This text was obtained through a mirror site, so the figures need to be checked against the original ([The Astana Times](https://astanatimes.com/2025/09/kazakh-citizens-split-on-ais-role-in-education-new-survey-shows/)).

## Evidence on procrastination in Kazakhstan rests on one study with n = 72

Peer-reviewed evidence on procrastination in Kazakhstan is very limited. The only study whose content was checked is the article "Academic Procrastination of Masters of the University in the Conditions of the COVID-19 Pandemic", published in 2021 in the Bulletin of Al-Farabi Kazakh National University (*Journal of Educational Sciences*, 66(1), 35–40, DOI 10.26577/JES.2021.v66.i1.04). During the switch to remote learning, 72 students were assessed with a modified Tuckman Procrastination Scale: **50% scored high, 43% medium and 7% low** ([KazNU Bulletin](https://bulletin-pedagogic-sc.kaznu.kz/index.php/1-ped/en/article/view/939)). The article page does not list the authors. The title says "masters" while the abstract says "bachelors", and the paper does not state how the scale was modified, in which language it was used, or its reliability. Interviewed students with high scores named complex, large or unclear tasks, especially tasks lacking necessary information, as reasons for delay. The "50% high" figure should not be cited as a national prevalence: the sample is small, from one university, collected under COVID-19 conditions, and the cut-offs of a "modified" scale cannot be compared with other studies. Another Kazakhstani paper ("Research of procrastination in connection with students' personal characteristics (on the example of Kazakhstan)") was found only as a ResearchGate title ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/346136310_Research_of_procrastination_in_connection_with_students'_personal_characteristics_on_the_example_of_Kazakhstan)). The search was not exhaustive: the Karaganda University bulletin, the KazNU psychology series, the ENU bulletins, eLibrary and CyberLeninka were not searched systematically.

Several Russian-language instruments exist, but most of their psychometric data could not be verified. Zvereva (2015) adapted the PASS to a Russian sample (*Psychological Science and Education*, 20(1), 79–84). The project page mentions more than 1,000 respondents but gives no reliability or validity coefficients ([psy.su](https://psy.su/psyche/projects/2268/)). A short form of Lay's General Procrastination Scale was tested in Belarus with 1,911 respondents and showed "a stable two-factor model", but the abstract page gives neither coefficients nor the number of items ([BSU e-library](https://elib.bsu.by/handle/123456789/317434)). The Russian adaptation of Steel's PPS and IPS (Klepikova & Kormacheva, 2019) was confirmed at title level only ([systempsychology.ru](http://systempsychology.ru/journal/2019_31/647-n-m-klepikova-i-n-kormacheva-adaptaciya-shkaly-prokrastinacii-i-shkaly-irracionalnoy-prokrastinacii-v-metodike-p-stila.html)). **No procrastination scale validated in Kazakh was found at all**, and there is no Russian or Kazakh validation of the Tuckman scale either. Regional studies of the digital environment are small. In Russia, Marinova et al. (2025) used the PASS, the Chen Internet Addiction Scale and the Academic Motivation Scale with 89 students and found that procrastinating students scored higher on internet-addiction factors ([psyjournals.ru](https://psyjournals.ru/en/journals/bppe/archive/2025_n2/Marinova_Feoktistova_et_al)). In Uzbekistan, Samadova & Anarbayev (2026) studied 30 students with their own questionnaire and found no significant relation between self-regulated learning and procrastination (r(28) = −.12, p = .53) ([Sindex.uz](https://sindex.uz/kk/article/200849)). This result shows that a validated scale and an adequate sample are needed to detect weak effects.

The origin and psychometrics of the UPS were confirmed from the full text. The authors are Fernie, Bharucha, Nikčević & Spada (2017), and the article appeared in the *Journal of Rational-Emotive & Cognitive-Behavior Therapy* (35, 136–149, DOI 10.1007/s10942-016-0247-x, online 11 August 2016). C. Marino, who is named in some sources, is not an author of this article ([Kingston eprint](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/38229/1/Nikcevic_A_38229_VoR.pdf); [LSBU](https://openresearch.lsbu.ac.uk/item/872w4)). In the first study (139 adults), one component explained 60.7% of the variance with **α = .89**, exactly the value found in the project. In the second study (155 adults), CFA confirmed a **one-factor, six-item** scale after one item was dropped (χ²(9) = 10.72, RMSEA = .038, CFI = .99, TLI = .99). The response format has four points and the total ranges from 6 to 24 ([Kingston eprint](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/38229/1/Nikcevic_A_38229_VoR.pdf)). The validity data are decisive for interpretation. The UPS correlates **ρ = .78** with the General Procrastination Scale, only ρ = .25 with intentional delay (IDP), ρ = .53 with depression (PHQ-9) and ρ = .47 with anxiety (GAD-7) ([Kingston eprint](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/38229/1/Nikcevic_A_38229_VoR.pdf)). The UPS therefore works in practice like a general (trait) procrastination scale, and affective distress may well be an unadjusted confounder of the GenAI–UPS association. The original samples were adult community samples, mostly women and white British, not students. **No Russian or Kazakh translation or validation of the UPS, and no use of it in Central Asia, was found.** For a Turkish adaptation there is only a record in a university repository ([Atatürk University AVESIS](https://avesis.atauni.edu.tr/publication/details/07208a9a-c65c-48b1-b1d8-ebddf8d45d66/turkish-adaptation-of-the-unintentional-procrastination-scale-validity-and-reliability-studies)).

## International evidence repeats the size of ρ = 0.28, not causality

Studies from 2024–2026 repeat the size and direction of the Indian result but do not support a causal conclusion. The table below summarises comparable effect sizes. No meta-analysis combining GenAI and procrastination specifically was found, so there is no pooled r.

| Study | Country, design, sample | GenAI measure | Procrastination measure | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sharma et al. (2026) data, project analysis ([Mendeley Data](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/88pv88j8jb/1)) | India, cross-sectional, n = 285 | 4-level academic use frequency | UPS, 6 items, α = 0.89 | ρ = 0.28 [0.18, 0.38]; β = 0.29 |
| Abbas, Jam & Khan (2024), *IJETHE* ([Table 5](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-024-00444-7/tables/5)) | Pakistan affiliation, three waves, n = 494 | 8-item ChatGPT usage scale | Scale not verified | β = 0.309 (t = 6.984, p < .001) |
| Galindo-Domínguez et al. (2026), *RIED* ([PDF](https://revistas.uned.es/index.php/ried/article/download/45497/33563/148999)) | Spain, cross-sectional, N = 467 | ChatGPT Dependence Scale (Ye et al., 2024) | Yockey (2016), 5 items | r = .231; β = .237 [.140, .334] |
| Liu & Liu (2026), *Frontiers in Psychology* ([Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1859317/full)) | China, cross-sectional, N = 1,869 | Perceived usefulness of GenAI; GenAI dependence | Academic procrastination | r = .324; r = .338 |
| Tang & Thang (2026), *IJERE* ([IJERE](https://ijere.iaescore.com/index.php/IJERE/article/view/38954)) | Probably Vietnam, PLS-SEM, n = 301 | AI overreliance | Academic procrastination | β = 0.430 |
| Zhao et al. (2026), *Frontiers in Psychology* ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13370561/)) | China, three-wave panel, n = 1,200 → 788 | GenAI use frequency (T1) | Procrastination (T1 controlled, T3) | β = 0.007 [−0.057, 0.070], not significant |
| Li, Huang & Cui (2026), *Behavioral Sciences* ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/16/7/1256)) | China, cross-sectional, n = 1,243 | 3-item frequency | GALP: putting off using GenAI for learning | Direct β = −0.195; total −0.278 |

The Indian confidence interval (.18–.38) contains the β = .309 of Abbas et al. (2024), the Spanish r = .231 and the Chinese r = .324–.338. The project therefore reproduces the international pattern in size and direction. Among 708 meta-analytic correlations collected by Gignac & Szodorai (2016), the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles were r = .11, .19 and .29, so **ρ = .28 counts as a "relatively large" effect for individual-differences research** ([FORRT](https://forrt.org/curated_resources/effect-size-guidelines-for-individual-di/)). Still, ρ² ≈ .08, meaning it explains only about 8% of the shared rank variance, and in the GenAI–procrastination literature this size is typical rather than unusual. Differences between constructs matter. Measures of dependence and overreliance give the largest effects (Tang & Thang, β = 0.430), which fits the idea that problematic use reflects procrastination better than plain frequency. The coefficient of Tang & Thang (2026) may be inflated by overlap between constructs, and the second author's name differs between records (Le, Q. T. and Thang, L. Q.). The negative β in Li et al. (2026) does not contradict the other studies, because it measures *putting off using GenAI for learning* rather than general academic procrastination ([MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/16/7/1256)).

Evidence on the direction of causality is weak and points both ways. Abbas et al. (2024) measured the predictors, ChatGPT use and the outcomes in three waves one to two weeks apart. Baseline procrastination was not controlled, however, and the authors themselves acknowledge that the relationship may run in both directions ([PsyPost](https://www.psypost.org/chatgpt-linked-to-declining-academic-performance-and-memory-loss-in-new-study/)). In the same study, workload (β = 0.133) and time pressure (β = 0.163) predicted ChatGPT use ([IJETHE Table 5](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-024-00444-7/tables/5)). This points to a likely common cause: workload can push a student both towards AI and towards procrastination. In the panel of Zhao et al. (2026), with T1 procrastination controlled, T1 frequency did not predict T3 procrastination (β = 0.007). Learning-centred use was related to later procrastination only indirectly, through self-regulated learning (−0.091) and self-efficacy (−0.024). Reverse paths were not tested, and the students who stayed in the panel had lower baseline procrastination ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13370561/)). Galindo-Domínguez et al. (2026) and Liu & Liu (2026) model the opposite direction, from procrastination to dependence (β = .237 and β = .224). Because the UPS describes procrastination as involuntary and trait-like, the explanation that "students prone to procrastination turn to GenAI" is no less plausible than "GenAI causes procrastination". Neither a cross-lagged panel that tests both directions nor an experiment that manipulates access to GenAI was found.

The Indian data have an important limitation of their own. The Mendeley Data record (v1, 23 March 2026, DOI 10.17632/88pv88j8jb.1) is titled "Datasheets for Role of Generative Artificial Intelligence Usage on Academic Dishonesty Among Indian Undergraduate and Postgraduate Students". The data were collected at CHRIST (Deemed to be) University, Delhi NCR, by convenience sampling through WhatsApp and LinkedIn ([Mendeley Data](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/88pv88j8jb/1)). The project's procrastination analysis is therefore a secondary analysis of variables collected for another purpose (a study of academic dishonesty). The author order in the DOI registry (DataCite) is Sharma, Nandy, Ramchandani, James, Batra, Dhanda, so the citation "Sharma et al. (2026)" is correct; a full reference should list all six authors. The dataset also contains the ChatGPT scale of Abbas et al. (2024) and the cognitive-offloading items of Gerlich (2025) ([Mendeley Data](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/88pv88j8jb/1)). This lets the project replace the 4-level frequency item with the multi-item Abbas scale to check the robustness of the result, and test offloading as a moderator. Depression and anxiety were not measured, so they cannot be controlled in the Indian data.

## The Indian result must pass four filters before it applies to Kazakhstan

The first argument for transfer to Kazakhstan is **consistent replication across countries**. A positive association of roughly the same size was found in a Pakistan-affiliated sample, in Spain, in China and in a probable Vietnamese sample, so it is not a phenomenon specific to India. Use in Kazakhstan is at least as high, and regional studies (Aubakirov, 2026; Zaripova et al., 2026) show that frequency alone is only weakly related to outcomes, which fits a moderate correlation such as ρ ≈ .3. Even so, the result does not transfer directly. Four filters stand in the way, and each requires a different adjustment.

The first filter is **range restriction**. If 65% of students used AI weekly in 2024 and later samples contain almost no non-users ([Pedagogy and Psychology](https://journal-pedpsy.kaznpu.kz/index.php/ped/article/view/1801); [Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1809360/full)), answers to a 4-level frequency item will cluster in the top categories. Variance shrinks and the correlation weakens. This is a statistical inference, but it leads to a concrete prediction: **a frequency-based ρ in Kazakhstan will probably be lower than the Indian 0.28**, and that would not mean the association is absent. The second filter is **institutional normalisation**. When the state makes AI courses compulsory and reaches almost all students through AI-Sana ([bilim.expert](https://www.bilim.expert/en/post/dinara-shcheglova-ai-sana-shaping-a-new-paradigm-for-higher-education)), frequency starts to reflect programme requirements more than individual differences. The information relevant to procrastination moves from frequency to dependence and type of use. One can therefore expect associations based on dependence and offloading to be as large in Kazakhstan as the international .23–.34 or larger. This is a hypothesis to be tested.

The third filter is **language and measurement**. There is no Kazakh or Russian version of the UPS, and α = .89 was obtained only in an English-language adult sample and in Indian students ([Kingston eprint](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/38229/1/Nikcevic_A_38229_VoR.pdf)). Differences between language groups are real. In the trilingual survey of Issakhanova et al. (2026), self-rated resilience differed by language group (χ²(2) = 18.43, p < .001, Cramér's V = .237): about 60% of Kazakh-language respondents rated their resilience as high, yet 50% of Kazakh-speaking interviewees said they were embarrassed to discuss psychological problems. The authors interpret this as culturally conditioned under-reporting ([Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1838533/pdf)). If procrastination is under-reported in the same way, the correlation in the Kazakh-language group may be artificially weakened. Language groups therefore cannot be compared without measurement invariance. The language of instruction may itself be a moderator: if GenAI works less well in Kazakh, frequency for students in Kazakh-language programmes also includes the time and effort spent on translation. No source has tested this quantitatively.

The fourth filter is **confounders and sample composition**. The Indian β = 0.29 is adjusted only for age, gender and education. It barely differs from ρ = 0.28, so the demographic variables do not explain the association. However, the UPS's associations with depression (.53) and anxiety (.47) ([Kingston eprint](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/38229/1/Nikcevic_A_38229_VoR.pdf)) and the prediction of ChatGPT use by workload and time pressure ([IJETHE Table 5](https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/articles/10.1186/s41239-024-00444-7/tables/5)) point to more important confounders, which must be measured in Kazakhstan. The sample composition offers a special opportunity: 9,300 Indian students study in Kazakhstan ([24.kz](https://24.kz/ru/news/obrazovanie-i-nauka/702285-kazakhstanskie-vuzy-privlekli-rekordnoe-kolichestvo-studentov)). They should not be mixed with local students. Their cultural background is close to the Sharma sample while their institutional environment is Kazakhstani, so treating them as a separate stratum would help to partly separate the effects of culture and institutional context. Intentional delay also matters: the UPS captures it only weakly (ρ = .25 with IDP). A student who knows that AI can draft a project quickly may consciously postpone the task, and the UPS would not detect such strategic delay.

## Next study: UPS in two languages, three waves, 365+ students

The first stage should adapt the instrument, because a Kazakh and Russian version of the UPS would be a scientific contribution in itself. The UPS should be translated into Kazakh and Russian with forward and back translation following the ITC guidelines, followed by cognitive interviews, a CFA test of the one-factor six-item structure, and a test of measurement invariance between the Kazakh and Russian versions. Issakhanova et al. (2026) showed that a trilingual survey is feasible at a Kazakhstani university ([Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2026.1838533/pdf)). Convergent validity is best tested against the short form of Lay's GPS in Russian ([BSU e-library](https://elib.bsu.by/handle/123456789/317434)), because the original ρ = .78 gives a concrete point of comparison. Discriminant validity can be tested with the IDP scale of the Active Procrastination Scale ([Kingston eprint](https://eprints.kingston.ac.uk/38229/1/Nikcevic_A_38229_VoR.pdf)). The Turkish adaptation can serve as a model in a Turkic language, but its content has not been verified, and Turkish is no substitute for Kazakh.

GenAI should be measured in several layers, not one. For direct comparison with the Sharma data, the 4-level academic use-frequency item should be kept. For comparison with the β = .309 of Abbas et al. (2024), their 8-item ChatGPT scale should be added ([Mendeley Data](https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/88pv88j8jb/1)). Dependence can be measured with the ChatGPT Dependence Scale (Ye et al., 2024) used by Galindo-Domínguez et al. (2026) ([RIED PDF](https://revistas.uned.es/index.php/ried/article/download/45497/33563/148999)). Type of use is best measured with the Kazakhstani categories (summarising, idea generation, essay writing, using AI as the main information source, submitting lightly edited text) plus translation. The language in which students work with GenAI should also be recorded. Covariates should include PHQ-9 and GAD-7 (as in the UPS validation), workload and time pressure (Abbas et al.), self-regulated learning (Zhao et al.; Zaripova et al.), language of instruction and of the questionnaire, year of study, citizenship, participation in AI-Sana and the type of the university's AI policy. To reduce self-report bias, LMS submission times relative to deadlines should be added as a behavioural indicator. In an environment with detectors and originality thresholds, students may hide that they submit lightly edited AI text, so guarantees of anonymity and confidentiality are a condition for honest answers.

The design should be longitudinal and multi-site. Following Zhao et al. (2026), three waves about four weeks apart are appropriate ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13370561/)), but the waves should be coordinated with midterms and the exam schedule. A cross-lagged panel model that tests both directions at once would fill the largest gap in the international literature. Universities should be chosen to differ in AI policy: NU with a formal policy, KazNU with hybrid rules (75% originality), and Narxoz or Yessenov, which had no AI rules in May 2026 ([Times of Central Asia](https://timesca.com/ai-in-kazakh-universities/)). Because rules are changing quickly, each university's current policy should be checked again at the time of fieldwork. For sample size, a Fisher z approximation (α = .05, two-sided, 80% power) gives about **98** complete cases for ρ = .28, about **240** for ρ = .18 (the lower bound of the Indian confidence interval) and about **783** if the effect in Kazakhstan weakens to ρ = .10. These numbers rise slightly for Spearman's coefficient. Taking the 65.7% retention of the Zhao et al. panel into account, about **365** students are needed at the first wave for 240 complete cases to remain at the third wave. If the Kazakh- and Russian-language groups need separate estimates, the total is about 730 students. The hypotheses, the analysis plan and the primary metric, Spearman's ρ with a 95% CI for direct comparison with the Indian result, should be preregistered.

## Conclusion

For Kazakhstan, the main question is not whether ρ ≈ .28 replicates. What matters is what use frequency measures in an environment where the state makes AI literacy compulsory and universities police originality with detectors. In such an environment frequency may become an institutional variable rather than an individual difference, and the information relevant to procrastination lies in dependence, offloading and self-regulation. If a Kazakhstani study finds a weaker frequency-based correlation than in India, that would not refute the Indian result. Instead, it would support the explanation suggested by the Zhao et al. panel and the Zaripova et al. regression: what matters is how students use AI, not how often. The most lasting scientific contribution of the project may be the first Kazakh and Russian version of the UPS in Central Asia rather than the correlation itself.

The policy implications are clear. The most rigorous evidence shows that frequency does not predict later procrastination, and two teaching interventions in Kazakhstan (Kani, 2024; Tuxanbayev et al., 2026, ENU and Astana International University) improved critical thinking and media literacy ([NU Repository](https://nur.nu.edu.kz/items/b3f800c9-a837-46ca-beef-57acd77fcad9/full); [Mendeley](https://www.mendeley.com/catalogue/35b9f9c7-48b4-31f5-ae1b-b255af1c68f8/)). This is consistent with the MSHE's position against a ban and supports adding self-regulation and learning-oriented AI use to the compulsory AI courses. The scientific basis for restricting access is weak. The 9,300 Indian students in Kazakhstan offer a rare natural comparison: with the cultural background held constant, one can test whether the institutional context changes the association. No single-country study can answer that question.
